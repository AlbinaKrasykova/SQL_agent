Nutrition and hormones — short reference (sample; replace with your own PDFs)

Protein and hormones
Adequate protein supports stable blood sugar and satiety hormones such as leptin and ghrelin balance over the day. Very low protein intake may coincide with higher hunger and mood variability in some people.

Carbohydrates and insulin
Refined carbohydrates can cause rapid glucose spikes and insulin responses. Whole grains and fiber-rich carbs tend to produce slower, smaller glucose curves, which is easier on energy and mood for many individuals.

Fats and hormones
Healthy fats (olive oil, nuts, fatty fish) provide building blocks for steroid hormones including sex hormones and cortisol regulation pathways. Extremely low-fat diets are not necessary for most people and may affect hormone production if sustained long term.

Caffeine and cortisol
Caffeine can temporarily raise cortisol and alertness. Late-day caffeine may delay sleep; poor sleep often worsens next-day cortisol rhythm, mood, and appetite hormones.

Sleep and hormones
Growth hormone and many repair processes are tied to deep sleep. Short sleep is linked in research to higher ghrelin (hunger), insulin resistance, and stress hormone dysregulation.

Meal timing
Regular meal timing helps anchor circadian rhythms. Skipping meals then overeating may produce larger glucose swings, which some people perceive as energy crashes or irritability.

Fiber and gut hormones
Fiber feeds gut bacteria that influence GLP-1 and other signals related to fullness. Vegetables, legumes, and whole grains are common sources.

Sugar and mood
Large sugar loads without protein or fat may cause reactive hypoglycemia in sensitive individuals, sometimes felt as anxiety or low mood 1–3 hours after eating.

Personal tracking
Combining food logs, mood logs, and sleep or step data helps you notice personal patterns. General nutrition science does not replace individual medical advice.
